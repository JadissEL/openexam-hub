The German exam you should book depends on **why** you need the certificate. A visa, a university place and a licence to practise are three different procedures, and they do not all ask for the same test.

This page sets out the official CEFR levels and the exams named on government and university-framework pages, checked on 21 November 2026. It is not legal advice, and it does not promise a score. Rules can differ by German mission, university or federal state (Land). Confirm the requirement that applies to you before you register.

## Start with the purpose

- **A visa or residence title.** The Residence Act sets a legal minimum for some visa types and none for others. The German mission that takes your application, or a recognition procedure, may still ask for more.
- **A German-taught degree.** Universities usually want a higher-education language exam such as TestDaF or DSH, or an exam the framework treats as an equivalent.
- **Professional recognition**, especially in healthcare. You may need general German at a stated CEFR level **and**, separately, a profession-specific Fachsprachenprüfung.

A [Goethe-Zertifikat](/exams/goethe) is a common way to show a CEFR level for visas, naturalisation and general professional German. It is not automatically the exam a university or a medical chamber will ask for.

## Visas: what the Residence Act actually requires

[Make it in Germany](https://www.make-it-in-germany.com/fileadmin/1_Rebrush_2022/a_Fachkraefte/PDF-Dateien/3_Visum_u_Aufenthalt/Visagrafik_EN/Visum_erforderliche_Deutschkenntnisse_EN.pdf), the federal portal for skilled workers, publishes a graphic of “Required German Language Skills Depending on the Type of Visa According to the Residence Act (AufenthG).” The edition checked for this article is dated **January 2026**, from the Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Energy.

It is careful about the limit of that table: the figures are the **legal** requirements in the Residence Act. “Other language requirements may apply as part of any necessary recognition procedure or the examination procedure at the German missions abroad.”

![Two-column chart of German visa types: those with no legal language requirement, and those that set a CEFR level such as A1, A2, B1 or B2](/blog/german-exams-visa-university-professional-recognition/visa-language-levels.webp)

*Chart by OpenExam Hub. Source: Make it in Germany visa language graphic, as of January 2026. Checked 21 November 2026.*

In that graphic:

**No legal German requirement** is listed for a work visa for qualified professionals, the EU Blue Card, a work visa for professionally experienced workers (including IT specialists), self-employment, research, a visa for language acquisition, and a study-related internship EU.

**A CEFR level is set** for:

| Visa type | Legal language requirement |
|---|---|
| Chancenkarte (Opportunity Card, points system) | German at least at A1, **or** English at least at B2 |
| Visa for the recognition of foreign qualifications | German at least at A2 |
| Visa for employment in a recognition partnership | German at least at A2 |
| Visa for vocational training | German at least at B1 |
| Visa for seeking a vocational training place | German at least at B1 |
| Visa for studying | As a general rule, German at least at B2, depending on the study programme |
| Visa for seeking a place in higher education | The language requirements of the desired study programme |

“No legal requirement” is not the same as “German will not help.” Employers can still expect German, and a regulated profession can still require a certificate during recognition. It only means the Residence Act itself does not set a language level for that visa type.

### Family reunification is a separate rule

The January 2026 graphic does not cover family visas. The Federal Office for Migration and Refugees ([BAMF](https://www.bamf.de/SharedDocs/Anlagen/DE/MigrationAufenthalt/Ehegattennachzug/ehegattennachzug-en.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=8)) explains the usual rule for **spousal reunification from abroad**: you must prove at least basic German, meaning **CEFR A1**, before you arrive, normally when you apply for the visa.

BAMF says the application should include a language certificate based on a standardised test that meets the standards of the Association of Language Testers in Europe (ALTE). It currently names “Start Deutsch 1” of the Goethe-Institut or of telc GmbH, “Grundstufe Deutsch 1” of the Austrian language diploma (ÖSD), and TestDaF. The German mission alone decides whether your proof is accepted. If, at the interview, it is already clear that you have basic German, BAMF says no separate certificate is needed.

There are many exceptions. You do not normally have to prove German if, among other cases, your spouse holds an **EU Blue Card** or an ICT Card, or a residence title as a skilled worker with vocational or academic training (§ 18a or 18b of the Residence Act), or certain other titles listed in the leaflet (including some researchers and self-employed people). Other exemptions include some nationalities, a short stay, illness or disability, or a hardship case. Not every exception is listed. Ask the visa office that will handle your file.

The English BAMF leaflet checked for this article was **last revised in March 2023**. The skilled-worker titles it names are still in the Residence Act, but confirm the current rule with the mission before you book an exam.

## University admission: TestDaF, DSH and equivalents

Most degree programmes in Germany are taught in German, so you will often have to prove German when you apply. [DAAD](https://www.daad.de/en/studying-in-germany/living-in-germany/german-language/), the German Academic Exchange Service, notes the usual exceptions: many **international degree programmes** (you then need another language, usually English), some postgraduate programmes, and often a stay of only one or two semesters. Always ask the international office for your course.

The two higher-education exams DAAD highlights are:

- **TestDaF** (Test Deutsch als Fremdsprache), which you can sit in around 100 countries, including your home country.
- **DSH** (Deutsche Sprachprüfung für den Hochschulzugang), which you take **in Germany**, at many higher education institutions.

![Table of typical German-language proofs for German-taught degrees: TestDaF TDN 4, DSH-2, Goethe-Zertifikat C2, telc Deutsch C1 Hochschule and DSD II](/blog/german-exams-visa-university-professional-recognition/university-german-exams.webp)

*Chart by OpenExam Hub. Sources: TestDaF-Institut and the KMK/HRK framework (RO-DT, amended November 2025). Checked 21 November 2026.*

The Kultusministerkonferenz and the Hochschulrektorenkonferenz set this out in the [Rahmenordnung über deutsche Sprachprüfungen (RO-DT)](https://www.testdaf.de/fileadmin/testdaf/downloads/Dokumente/2004_06_25-RO_DT.pdf), as amended on 4 November 2025 (HRK) and 27 November 2025 (KMK). The [TestDaF-Institut](https://www.testdaf.de/de/hochschulen/der-testdaf-und-hochschulen/nachweis-der-deutschkenntnisse-fuer-das-studium/) quotes the same rule.

For **unrestricted admission or enrolment in all programmes and degrees**, the framework treats as sufficient:

- **TestDaF** with at least **TDN 4 in every part** (reading, listening, writing and speaking). TestDaF reports TDN 3, 4 and 5. TDN 5 is above the usual admission level. A university may accept a lower result, such as TDN 3 in one skill, for a particular programme; that decision does not automatically travel with you if you change course or university.
- **DSH** passed with an overall result of at least **DSH-2**. A university may set DSH-1 or DSH-3 for particular purposes.
- The German paper of the **Feststellungsprüfung** at a Studienkolleg (or the body designated under Land law).
- **DSD II** (Deutsches Sprachdiplom of the Standing Conference, second level), which is aimed at pupils at German-language schools abroad.

You are also exempt from those four proofs if you already hold one of the “befreiende Prüfungen” in § 8 of the framework, including:

- **Goethe-Zertifikat C2: Großes Deutsches Sprachdiplom (GDS)**
- **ÖSD Zertifikat C2**
- **telc Deutsch C1 Hochschule**
- a school leaving certificate that is equivalent to a German higher-education entrance qualification

DAAD adds that some programmes also recognise **Goethe-Zertifikat B2 or C1**, and ÖSD Zertifikat B2 or C1. That is a programme-level choice, not the unrestricted default. If you completed the school leaving certificate that gives university entrance at a German-language school abroad, DAAD says you will not be required to take a language proficiency test.

TestDaF exists in a digital and a paper-based version. The framework says both measure the same levels. Fees depend on the country (TestDaF) or the institution (DSH); this article does not quote a price, because it varies.

English-taught programmes usually do not ask for German, but they do ask for English. Check the admissions page for the exact certificate and score.

## Professional recognition, doctors and nurses

[Anerkennung in Deutschland](https://www.anerkennung-in-deutschland.de/html/de/pro/deutschkenntnisse.php), the federal portal on recognition of foreign professional qualifications, makes a useful distinction. For **many regulated professions**, such as doctor or nurse, a stated level of German is required for the licence, and therefore for recognition. For **most other occupations**, recognition of the qualification itself does not require a particular language certificate, even if German will still help you find work.

The level is set in CEFR terms (A1 to C2). The portal’s [Anerkennungs-Finder](https://www.anerkennung-in-deutschland.de/) is the official way to see what applies to your profession and Land. You can often submit the language certificate **after** the recognition procedure has started.

In healthcare there are usually **two** proofs. The portal’s English page on [documents for the application](https://www.anerkennung-in-deutschland.de/html/en/documents-application.php) says that general German at a stated CEFR level may be shown with a certificate such as a Goethe-Institut diploma, and that additional **specialist language** is sometimes needed for authorisation to practise. That specialist proof is the **Fachsprachenprüfung**.

The federal [glossary](https://www.anerkennung-in-deutschland.de/html/de/service/glossar-f.php) describes the Fachsprachenprüfung (also spelled Fachsprachprüfung) as an oral and written test of profession-specific German, often including a patient conversation. The level depends on the profession. You do not usually register for it yourself; the competent authority tells you if you must take it. There can be exceptions, for example if you spent at least ten years at a German school and obtained a school leaving certificate, if the authority is already satisfied that you speak German, if your degree is from a German-language university, or if you completed at least three years of vocational training in German.

![Two official examples: doctors in Berlin need general German at B2 and usually a Fachsprachenprüfung at C1; nurses in Saarland need B2 plus a Fachsprachenprüfung](/blog/german-exams-visa-university-professional-recognition/healthcare-recognition.webp)

*Chart by OpenExam Hub. Source: Anerkennung in Deutschland finder PDFs for doctors in Berlin and nurses in Saarbrücken, downloaded 21 November 2026. The Fachsprachenprüfung is not a Goethe exam.*

Two official examples, both downloaded from the portal on 21 November 2026:

- **Doctors (Ärztin/Arzt) in Berlin.** You need general German at **B2**. You usually also have to take a Fachsprachenprüfung at **C1**. There are exceptions; the competent authority will tell you. You do not have to present a language certificate when you first apply; you can prove German later. The procedure named here is the granting of the **Approbation** (the unrestricted licence to practise).
- **Nurses (Pflegefachperson) in Saarbrücken, Saarland.** You need general German at **B2**, and a Fachsprachenprüfung for profession-specific German. The portal lists exceptions if German is your first language, or if you completed your vocational training in German. The competent authority contacts you if you must take the specialist exam. The procedure named here is permission to use the professional title.

Those are Land-specific results, not a single national score. Check the Finder for the Land where you intend to work. A Goethe-Zertifikat B2 can be a way to show the **general** B2 requirement if the authority accepts it; it does not replace the Fachsprachenprüfung.

The same portal notes that a visa to enter Germany for a recognition measure usually requires at least A2 — which matches the Residence Act table above. Language requirements for the **licence** are often higher than the visa minimum.

## Naturalisation is B1, and it is not a visa rule

If you later apply for German citizenship, [BAMF](https://www.bamf.de/EN/Themen/Integration/ZugewanderteTeilnehmende/Einbuergerung/einbuergerung-node.html) lists “sufficient knowledge of German (level B1 of the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages)” among the standard naturalisation conditions (the English page is dated 30 June 2025).

A “Zertifikat Integrationskurs” can prove that B1 level if you reached B1 in the “Deutsch-Test für Zuwanderer” (DTZ). BAMF also publishes a [list of other recognised B1 certificates](https://www.bamf.de/SharedDocs/Anlagen/DE/Integration/SprachnachweiseZertifikate/nachweis-b1-zertifkat.pdf?__blob=publicationFile). The list checked for this article is dated **July 2023** and includes **Goethe-Zertifikat B1** (and the youth version), several telc B1 exams, ÖSD Zertifikat B1, DSD I at B1, and the DTZ at B1. Certificates from a university language centre’s own internal exam are not accepted on that list. Ask the naturalisation authority which proof it will take; the list is used in BAMF procedures and may not be the last word for every authority.

## Which exam should you actually book?

Match the certificate to the procedure, not to a general idea of “a German exam”:

- **Visa or family reunification.** Book a certificate at the CEFR level the visa or mission requires (A1, A2 or B1 in the table above, unless an exemption applies). For spouses, BAMF’s named ALTE examples include Goethe or telc Start Deutsch 1. The mission decides what it will accept.
- **A German-taught degree.** Plan for TestDaF (TDN 4 in all parts) or DSH-2, unless your university names a lower score or accepts Goethe-Zertifikat C2, telc Deutsch C1 Hochschule or DSD II. Sit TestDaF abroad if you need the certificate before you travel; sit DSH after you arrive, if that is what the institution offers.
- **A licence to practise**, especially for doctors and nurses. Use a recognised **B2** certificate for general German if that is what your Land asks for, and treat the **Fachsprachenprüfung** as a separate exam run by the competent authority.
- **Naturalisation.** Aim at **B1**, for example Goethe-Zertifikat B1 or a DTZ at B1, and confirm with the authority.
