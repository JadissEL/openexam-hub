Language exams are not only for people applying to university. Professionals use them to register with a regulator, to change jobs, or to meet a language requirement for immigration. The right time to start preparing depends on *why* you need the result, and on the official rules of the exam and the organisation that asked for it.

## Start from the official requirement, not from a generic timetable

Before you pick a study length, read the page that actually asks for the test:

- For **university**, that is the admissions or language-requirements page for the course.
- For **work**, that is the regulator, employer or professional body.
- For **immigration**, that is the government page for the programme. For Canadian Express Entry, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) publishes the list of accepted tests and how results are used.

Those pages tell you which exam is accepted, which module or level, and — if they say so — how recent the result must be. OpenExam Hub does not set those rules, and we will not invent a number of months that “always” works.

## If you need the exam for university

Universities usually set their own deadlines and minimum scores. Some accept a result that is already in hand; others want the test taken by a date in the admissions calendar. The useful order of work is:

1. Note the application deadline and the last date a result will be accepted.
2. Check, on the exam body’s site, how long a score remains valid, and how long it takes to get results after the test day.
3. Leave a buffer in case you need a second sitting.
4. Only then decide how many weeks of preparation you can realistically give it.

A study plan helps you use that time well. It cannot move a university deadline.

## If you need the exam for work or immigration

Professionals often have a different clock. A job offer, a licence application or an immigration intake can appear with less notice than a university cycle. Some official results are also time-limited. For example, CCI Paris states that a TEF Canada results certificate is valid for two years from the date of issue. IRCC explains how language tests are used for Express Entry on its own site — read that page for the current rule, not a summary you found elsewhere.

That validity window is a reason to avoid sitting the exam far too early, and also a reason not to leave it until the certificate might expire mid-application. The official pages in the sources list below are the ones to trust.

## A practical way to start

Once you know the exam, the score you need, and the date you are working towards:

- Be honest about your current level. A short placement check is safer than guessing.
- Prefer material published by the exam body or a public broadcaster, not unofficial copies of papers.
- Practice the actual papers of the exam (listening, reading, writing, speaking), not only general language lessons.
- If you work full time, a plan that fits around your week is more useful than a timetable written for a full-time student.

## What this article does not claim

We have not given a standard number of months to prepare, and we have not quoted pass rates, university rankings or “typical” scores. Those figures go out of date and often do not apply to you. When a date, a fee, a validity period or a required score matters, follow the official source and check the date it was last updated.
